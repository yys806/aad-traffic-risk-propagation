## Question and method

The paper asks whether network-level congestion propagation and dissipation can be described with a parsimonious spreading model rather than a full microscopic simulation. Links are classified using the speed ratio `λ_i(t)=v_i(t)/v_i^max` and a threshold `ρ` (Eq. 1–2, p. 2). The fraction of congested, recovered and free-flow links is modeled by an SIR-like ODE with propagation rate `β` and recovery rate `μ` (Eq. 3–5, p. 3); `kβ/μ` is interpreted as a basic reproductive number.

## Evidence and results

The authors fit the model to one month of speed data from six cities and to a calibrated mesoscopic Melbourne assignment model. Fig. 3 reports empirical city fits and `R0` behavior across thresholds; Fig. 4–5 compare simulated spreading and recovery. The result is a macroscopic description of link-state propagation, not a message intervention or vehicle-level causal mechanism.

## What is actually shown

The data support a useful aggregate propagation/recovery model under the chosen threshold and effective-contact assumptions. They do not show that congestion is caused by an information process, that individual vehicles receive or adopt messages, or that a local risk event propagates through a message chain.

## Limitations

Homogeneous mixing is a simplifying assumption; the congestion threshold `ρ`, effective degree `k`, demand profile and link-level state classification affect fitted rates. Network aggregation discards vehicle identity, message lifecycle, and causal intervention timing.

## Relation to AAD

This is a strong physical-network baseline and a direct threat to any claim that “network risk propagation” is new by itself. AAD would need an event-level information chain and an independent physical baseline beyond this type of aggregate propagation.
