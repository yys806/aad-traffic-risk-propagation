Abstract
Ensuring autonomous vehicle safety in mixed traffic re mains di fficult because 
human-driven vehicles can create rare and unpredictable interactions that are
ARTICLE IN PRESS

underrepresented in conventional training. Here we present GA2AD, a gen-
eralized adaptive adversarial training framework that generates challenging
but controllable driving scenarios for autonomous vehicle decision-making. The
framework treats the target autonomous vehicle model as a black box and trains
a background-vehicle model to create adversarial maneuvers, including cutting
in, hard braking and speeding. A hybrid risk field estimates the interaction risk
imposed by surrounding vehicles, while an activation function selects when adver-
sarial maneuvers should be applied. An adaptive switching module alternates
training between the target vehicle and the background-vehicle model according
to collision intervals, avoiding overly weak or overly severe adversarial condi-
tions. In simulation, GA2AD reduces collision rates by 55–70% and lowers the
95th percentile absolute jerk by 21–42%, with only small decreases in average
speed. Tests on an autonomous vehicle track using synchronized virtual traffic
further show improved safety and comfort under the tested adversarial condi-
tions. These results indicate that adaptive adversarial scenario generation can
improve autonomous vehicle robustness in complex mixed traffic.

Introduction
Rapid advancements in the autonomous vehicle industry have led to many commercial 
deployments, resulting in a new era of mixed traffic that combines both  Autonomous 
Vehicles (AVs) and Human-Driven Vehicles (HDVs). However, such a mixed traf-
fic p oses s ubstantial challenges,  p articularly i n t erms o f e nsuring s afety w ithin these 
heterogeneous traffic environments [1 ]. Sp ecifically, the  beh avior of HDV s inherently 
shows considerable variability due to the diverse nature of human drivers, some of 
whom may even engage in traffic vi olations. On  th e ot her ha nd, in troducing AVs 
fundamentally changes traffic dy namics, le ading to  ne w pa tterns in  tr affic flow, thus 
requiring human drivers to adapt their behaviors accordingly, which heightens the 
unpredictability associated with human drivers. Such a rise in behavioral discrep-
ancy substantially affects t he i nteractions b etween h eterogeneous v ehicles, thereby 
hampering safety in mixed traffic [2].
The safety challenges in mixed traffic flo ws mai nly ari se fro m the  behavioral 
variability of human-driven vehicles, their possible traffic ru le vi olations, an d insuf-
ficient i nteraction w ith a utonomous v ehicles. H uman-driven v ehicles m ay display 
unpredictable behaviors that vary across drivers, leading to irregular and sometimes 
hazardous traffic co nditions [3 ]. Th ey ma y al so co mmit tr affic infr actions, such  as 
speeding or disobeying traffic si gnals, wh ich di srupt tr affic orde r and chal lenge the 
programmed expectations of autonomous vehicles [4]. In addition, insufficient com-
munication and limited cooperative patterns between autonomous and human-driven 
vehicles can lead to misjudgments and conflicts [5].
Researchers have employed various methods to tackle the above issues, including 
assessing risks in mixed traffic [6–8], predicting behaviors of surrounding vehicles [9],

or further establishing game-theoretic models between AVs and nearby HDVs to make 
appropriate decision [10]. Nevertheless, owing to the curses of dimensionality and rarity 
of safety-critical events in mixed traffic situations, these approaches often lack robust-
ness, the ability to reliably handle complex and challenging behaviors, and are thus 
particularly vulnerable to corner cases, which present bottlenecks in their reliability 
and effectiveness [11].
To address the challenges mentioned above, adversarial methods have been widely 
studied to improve model robustness against perturbations and adversarial attacks, 
which is applied in various deep learning domains including computer vision [12, 13] 
and natural language processing [14]. In the context of autonomous driving, researchers 
have developed adversarial approaches [15] to increase the robustness of AVs across 
three key functional aspects, i.e. perception, decision-making, and actuation. These 
approaches are applicable to both modularization [9, 16, 17] and end-to-end [6] archi-
tectures. In terms of perception, adversarial methods, such as data poisoning, attack 
target sensor systems to test their robustness. In terms of actuation, disturbances 
are introduced to vehicle outputs (e.g., steering, acceleration, and braking) to assess 
response reliability under various conditions [18, 19]. In terms of decision-making which 
is the focus of this paper, adversarial reinforcement learning has emerged as a promis-
ing paradigm for improving model robustness by simulating worst-case disturbances 
during agent training [20, 21], which typically involve a generator and a discrimina-
tor like generative adversarial networks. The generator produces decisions, while the 
discriminator evaluates these decisions to differentiate b etween vulnerable  and  invul-
nerable decisions, in turn, guiding the generator in refining its decision-making strategy 
by identifying the weaknesses of the generator and applying adversarial actions [22]. 
Nonetheless, these conventional adversarial methods, including generative adversar-
ial network (GAN)-based approaches, often require access to target model’s internal 
states or gradients for training, as they rely on simultaneous updates and shared 
information between components, which limits their generalizability across different 
model architectures and scenarios. Additionally, directly applying adversarial attacks 
(e.g., aggressive maneuvers from background vehicles) without adaptive control over 
their adversarial intensity often causes AV safety mechanisms to fail, as non-adaptive 
attacks may be too weak to expose vulnerabilities or excessively strong to be handled 
safely by the AV model. This highlights the need for solutions that are broadly appli-
cable across various vehicle models and scenarios to promote robustness and enhance 
safety in mixed traffic environments [23–25].
In this work, we present a Generalized Adaptive Adversarial training frame-
work for Autonomous Driving (GA2AD), an adaptive adversarial training method 
for improving autonomous vehicle robustness in mixed traffic. GA 2AD tr eats the 
target autonomous vehicle model as a black box and trains a background-vehicle 
model to generate challenging but controllable adversarial interactions. The frame-
work combines a hybrid-risk-field-based inverse risk assessment, an activation function 
for selecting appropriate adversarial conditions, and an adaptive switching mecha-
nism that alternates training between the target vehicle and the background vehicles 
according to collision intervals. We evaluate GA2AD in simulation across multiple

loop field t ests w ith s ynchronized v irtual t raffic. The  res ults sho w red uced collision

Results
GA2AD framework
In this study, we propose the GA2AD, which constructs adversarial scenarios where an 
adversary agent strategically manipulates interactions between vehicles. This adver-
sarial approach treats any reinforcement learning (RL)-based AV model, namely the 
Target Vehicle (TV) model, as a black box, and focuses on enhancing its defensive 
capabilities against adversarial actions as shown in Fig. 1a. Notably, the scope of 
GA2AD focuses on proactive collision avoidance and risk mitigation during inter-
actions, rather than assessing GA2AD’s performance impact on post-crash outcome 
severity (e.g., ∆v or injury risk).
Specifically, we define vehicles that surround the TV  and interact with it as Back-
ground Vehicles (BVs), as shown in the top left corner of Fig. 1b. Among these BVs, an 
Adversarial Background Vehicle (ABV) is dynamically selected to perform adversarial 
maneuvers, creating challenging scenarios to test and enhance the TV’s robustness. 
A corresponding BV model is proposed to dynamically adjust the maneuvers of BVs, 
which consists of two parts, the RL-based BV model and the rule-based BV model. 
At each simulation timestep, our GA2AD utilizes the rule-based BV model to con-
trol BVs at first to establish  a normal training environment. And the ABV candidate 
and the corresponding ABV’s maneuver are determined by the RL-based BV model. 
Additionally, the inverse risk assessment module and the activation function module 
serve as supplements to the BV model, as shown in Fig. 1b.
To evaluate whether the ABV candidate is appropriate, the framework introduces 
an inverse risk assessment to analyze the risk of each BV on the TV. In detail, the 
hybrid risk field considering states of the TV is proposed  based on the traditional risk 
field a s s hown i n F ig. 1 c, w hich a llows f or a  m ore p recise a ssessment o f t he individ-
ual risks of BVs to TV. The Bayesian Theory is utilized to calculate the individual 
risk of the ABV candidate and the holistic risk jointly caused by all BVs using the 
predefined p rior p robability o f d ifferent ri sk le vels, na mely da ngerous, at tentive and 
safe. The individual risk of the ABV candidate and the holistic risk are then fed into 
the activation function, ensuring it neither too conservative nor too aggressive. If the 
result of the activation function falls between the predefined upper and lower bounds, 
the ABV’s original maneuver will be replaced by an adversarial maneuver such as cut 
in, hard braking and speeding. This induces a complex adversarial scenario that chal-
lenges the TV’s robustness against both direct threats and the resulting environmental 
perturbations. These interactions are used as training inputs for the BV model only 
when the ABV performs adversarial maneuvers, namely f(ϵb, ϵenv) = 1 in Eq. (21), 
which ensures that the BV model learns from challenging and risky scenarios. Since a 
too conservative ABV can hinder the TV learning process, whereas overly aggressive

The training process for the TV and BV models, formulated as a two-player Markov 
Decision Process (MDP), is inherently prone to instability. To address this issue, we 
propose an adaptive switch module (ASM), as illustrated in Fig. 1a, which enables an 
alternating training mechanism between the TV and BV models. During each training 
phase, when the TV model is being updated, the BV model freezes its parameters, 
and vice versa. This design reduces the interference caused by simultaneous policy 
updates, a well-known challenge in multi-agent reinforcement learning that can lead 
to unstable training and convergence issues [26, 27].
In doing so, the multi-agent problem effectively transforms into a single-agent RL 
task, enhancing both the stability and timeliness of the training process for the two 
models. Meanwhile, to further ensure that the BV model does not become overly 
aggressive or excessively conservative, the adaptive switch module also leverages the 
collision episode interval (defined as the episode between two successive TV collisions

during training process) as a key switching criterion. In Fig. 1d, the module segments 
the collision rate curve into two regions at a threshold made of collision episode inter-
val: the above for the TV model training and the below for updating the BV model. 
By dynamically adjusting the BV’s adversarial intensity based on the TV’s safety per-
formance measured through the collision episode interval, the framework ultimately 
improves the training efficiency an d sa feguards th e TV fr om be ing ov erwhelmed by 
excessively aggressive adversarial behaviors. Specifically, with such a mechanism, when 
the TV experiences frequent collisions, the system is able to reduce adversarial inten-
sity by prioritizing TV training and limiting the aggressiveness of the ABVs. This 
mechanism ensures stable learning progress and prevents TV from being exposed to 
unrealistic or unmanageable threats during early stages of training.
Based on the normal training environment only with the rule-based BV model, this 
study proposes an adaptive adversarial training framework GA2AD that integrates 
RL-based BV model as the adversarial maneuvers generator, along with an activation 
function with the inverse risk assessment as the adversarial maneuvers discriminator 
to create an appropriate adversarial training environment for the TV model.
Training dynamics
This section analyzes the alternative training process of the TV and BV models under 
the ASM in the GA2AD framework. Through alternating training cycles, the TV 
progressively adapts to the adversarial strategies of the BV model, which concur-
rently enhances its own adversarial capabilities. The evaluation metrics reveal how the 
framework enhances TV performance by introducing increasingly challenging adver-
sarial scenarios. As shown in Fig. 2, the red curves indicate training processes for BV, 
whereas the blue curves represent those for the TV, illustrating differential strategies 
of alternating training processes and performance metrics over training episodes.
As shown in Fig. 2a, at the beginning of the training process I, the collision rate of 
the TV model is high. As the collision rate gradually decreases, the adaptive switch 
function is activated, initiating the training for the BV model, which leads to an 
increase in the collision rate again. This increase in the frequency of collision triggers 
the adaptive switch function once more, prompting the further training of the TV 
model. This results in a renewed decrease in the collision rate. This cycle continues 
until the training converges.
Fig. 2b illustrates a clear pattern of the average speed of the TV during the 
alternating training process: each time the BV model undergoes training, there is a 
noticeable decrease in the TV’s average speed. Initially, the average speed gradually 
increases with the training process of the TV model, but it soon experiences a rapid 
decline in speed during the first training session I of the BV model,  illustrating direct 
disruption of ABV’s maneuvers on the TV, and introducing new challenges or obstacles 
that temporarily reduce its speed. As the TV model resumes its own second training 
process II, the average speed generally increases, reflecting periods  of adjustment and 
improvement in its driving algorithms. Notably, the marked speed decline during the 
BV part of the third training process III suggests that the challenges posed by the BV 
model’s training grow more complex and adversarial as the training progresses.

alternating training mechanism allows each model to continually refine its  strategies, 
enhancing the overall performance and robustness of the framework.

Cross-validation
This section presents a comparison of TV model performance metrics across different 
training and testing strategies. We define t he t raining p rocess o f t he G A2AD a s the 
adversarial training process. After adversarial training, the trained BV model contin-
ues to construct the adversarial test process. In contrast, the training process that no 
BV model is trained, which consists of only the default setting, is considered as a nor-
mal training process. Similarly, the testing process without the trained BV model is the 
normal testing process. Then we compared the TV performances between normal and 
adversarial training under the normal and adversarial testing conditions. As shown in 
Fig. 4, we consider four combinations of training and testing processes: NN represents 
Normal Training + Normal Testing, serving as the baseline of the TV performance 
under the conditions of normal training and testing; AN denotes Adversarial Training
+ Normal Testing, indicating enhanced performance of the TV model due to adver-
sarial training methods during the normal testing; NA indicates Normal Training + 
Adversarial Testing, indicating performance drops of the normally trained TV due to 
insufficient responses for adversarial conditions; AA corresponds to Adversarial Train-
ing + Adversarial Testing, underlining the robustness advantage of the adversarially 
trained TV in challenging scenarios of adversarial testing.

achieves lower collision rates than those trained in normal environments (refer to 
NN and NA). Notably, the baseline NN collision rate (1 × 10−6 /km) is lower than 
real-world benchmarks from China’s Demonstration Zones (≈ 6.48 × 10−6 /km) [28].

Generalization analysis
We further assess the generalization of the GA2AD framework to the TV model 
which varies on different R L a lgorithms. T o d emonstrate t he G A2AD framework’s 
compatibility with diverse state-of-the-art RL approaches for AV decision-making, we 
evaluated TV models by employing different deep  reinforcement learning  algorithms. 
In addition to the off-policy a nd value-based D eep Q -Network ( DQN) a lgorithm, we 
analyzed the model performance using off-policy and actor-critic methods such as the 
Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) [29], the Soft Actor-Critic 
(SAC) [30], and the Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) [31]. These evalu-
ations were conducted in both normal and adversarial test environments to compare 
the performance gap between adversarially and normally trained TV models across 
different algorithms using the four performance  indicators defined in Evaluation met-
rics. The gap represents the difference in TV performance  metrics between adversarial 
training (AA, AN) and normal training (NN, NA) in terms of various RL-algorithm 
TV models, i.e., Gap in Adversarial Test = Metric(NA) - Metric(AA), Gap in Normal 
Test = Metric(NN) - Metric(AN). A larger gap in collision rate, the 95th percentile 
absolute jerk, environmental risk indicates greater improvement about safety and com-
fort due to adversarial training. And a smaller gap in average speed indicates loss of 
operational efficiency.
Fig. 5 illustrates the differences o n t he f our m etrics w hen t he R L a lgorithms for 
controlling the TV are DQN, TD3, SAC, and DDPG. Since the differences between 
the studied RL algorithms are small, the results suggest the generalizability of the 
GA2AD adversarial training framework. In both normal and adversarial testing envi-
ronments, the GA2AD consistently achieves better results in collision rate, the 95th 
percentile absolute jerk, and environmental risk, affirming its capability  of enhancing 
safety and stability for different m odels. M oreover, u nder a dversarial t esting, these 
three metrics exhibit greater reductions compared to normal testing, indicating that 
the framework achieves improved safety and driving comfort in adversarial scenarios.

Ablation study
The adaptive switch module in the GA2AD framework dynamically adjusts the train-
ing durations of the BV and TV models based on the TV’s safety performance, in 
contrast to fixed-time-slot methods like those in [20]. Fig. 6a illustrates the reward per-
formance of the TV model under different training  setups,  including normal  training 
(NT), the proposed GA2AD, and three fixed-switch-interval adversarial training vari-
ants. Specifically, AT-100, AT-200, and AT-400 denote adversarial training variants in 
which the training process is alternately switched between the TV and BV models at 
fixed intervals of 100, 200, and 400 episodes,  respectively. The NT serves  as the  base-
line, operating in a less hazardous environment without ABVs. Consequently, the TV 
in the NT environment achieves rapid convergence to a stable and high reward level 
early in the training process due to the absence of adversarial behaviors. In contrast, 
the GA2AD framework incorporates adversarial risk states introduced by ABVs, which 
initially cause a drop in the TV’s reward due to the challenging scenarios. However, 
through adaptive training, GA2AD enables the TV model to recover and achieve a 
stable reward level comparable to NT, demonstrating its ability to handle adversarial 
conditions effectively. Fixed-time-slot alternating training methods ( AT-100, AT-200, 
and AT-400) perform poorly, exhibiting lower rewards and unstable convergence even 
after extended training. The longer the fixed-time-slot i nterval ( e.g., A T-400), the 
worse the performance, as these methods fail to adapt to the TV’s learning needs, 
resulting in consistently low rewards and poor stability.
Fig. 6b depicts the environmental risk across different t raining s etups, w ith NT 
again serving as the baseline. NT exhibits the lowest environmental risk through-
out training due to the absence of adversarial behaviors from ABVs. In contrast, 
GA2AD introduces controlled adversarial scenarios, leading to higher initial environ-
mental risk. The fixed-time-slot m ethods ( AT-100, A T-200, a nd A T-400) r esult in 
higher environmental risk, particularly with the longest interval AT-400.
We selected AT-400 as the variant GA2AD without the adaptive switch module in 
the time-to-collision (TTC) distribution comparison shown in Fig. 6c, as it exhibits 
the most severe training instability, highest environmental risk, and poorest TV per-

contrast to illustrate the necessity of the collision-interval-based adaptive switching 
module in enhancing training effectiveness and stability. The TTC distribution results 
in Fig. 6c further support this choice, revealing that AT-400 generates overly aggressive 
scenarios with notably lower TTC values (mostly less than 1 s), indicating unstable 
and extreme adversarial conditions, whereas GA2AD maintains a more balanced dis-
tribution of TTC values, ensuring challenging yet manageable training scenarios that 
enhance TV robustness without compromising stability.

Discussion
Our results demonstrate that the GA2AD framework can increase the robustness and 
safety of AVs in mixed traffic through adversarial training. The dynamic  interactions 
between TV and adversarial BVs have been simulated to help the TV model adapting 
to adversarial scenarios. The activation function edits the MDP and accelerates the 
training process of the BV model, improving the training efficiency. Th e training 
process for the TV and BV models is also separated and alternated to dynamically 
adjust the adversarial intensity for enhancing the TV’s defensive capabilities. The 
TV model is treated as a black-box in this framework, which facilitates GA2AD’s 
generalization across the tested models as demonstrated by cross-comparison among 
variants of TV models.
While the GA2AD framework has demonstrated its efficacy in  robustness,  safety, 
comfort, timing efficiency, ge neralizability an d sc alability, se veral li mitations should 
be noted. Under the condition of the uncooperative interactions between the TV and 
BVs, a deep exploration into the game-theoretic equilibria under various traffic con-
ditions would provide valuable insights into the long-term stability and evolution of 
mixed traffic flows. Although such an equilibrium analysis is beyond the scope of  the 
current work due to the inherent limitations of simulation-based experiments, we pro-
pose to explore this direction in future research by incorporating real-world data and 
more advanced modeling techniques to derive and analyze equilibria, which could help 
predict emergent traffic behaviors and inform strategies for optimizing overall system

Methods
Problem formulation
This study tackles the issue of adversarial training in multi-agent driving environ-
ments by modeling the interaction between the TV agent, referred as α, and the

Background vehicle model
In this study, we model an adversarial environment for an AV, referred to as the TV,
focusing on its interactions with six surrounding BVs. As shown in the top left of the
Fig. 1b, we define the set of BVs as follows:
ΩB = {front, left − front, right − front, rear, left − rear
, right − rear}. (5)
We propose a BV model to control BVs located at these specific locations  relative 
to the TV. The BV model consists of two parts, namely the RL-based BV model and 
the rule-based BV model. At each time step, BVs are controlled by the rule-based 
model by default, which is programmed to follow predefined traffic rules. Unlike other 
research [41] that directly applies the rule-based AV model to refine a nd constrain 
decisions of the AV made by the RL-based AV model, this model establishes a stable 
and normal driving environment that mimics typical human driving patterns, allowing 
the TV to initially learn basic safe interactions in moderate traffic co nditions. The 
RL-based BV model is trained on the basis of the rule-based model to select the most 
suitable ABV candidate b ∈ B to carry out adversarial actions during the training 
process of the BV model. The RL-based BV model dynamically introduces adversarial 
scenarios, enhancing the TV’s ability to handle complex and challenging situations, 
thereby improving its robustness and safety performance.
In detail, the observation space of the RL-based BV model encompasses two main 
components. First, the TV’s states, including the lateral position (xt), speed (vt), 
acceleration (at), and heading angle (θt) are considered. Second, the states of the BVs 
are considered, including the relative distance (∆db), speed (vb), and acceleration (ab) 
of the BVs. The action space of the RL-based BV model includes two types of actions:

The inverse risk assessment module evaluates the potential risk posed by surround-

In the GA2AD framework, a mechanism termed the switch indicator CR is integrated
to adaptively adjust the training process between the TV and the BV. This switch is
governed by a threshold value (H), which is determined by our extensive experiments
with an automatic hyperparameter optimization software framework Optuna [51]. The
training target shifts towards the TV when CR > H , and to the BV when CR ≤ H.
The adoption of this mechanism mitigates instability issues inherent in multi-agent
frameworks by converting the training setup into a more stable single-agent context.
This process not only enhances the adaptability and learning efficiency throughout
different stages but also maintains the universality of the training, aiming to ensure
safety and robustness in various driving scenarios.

Evaluation metrics
Improving the safety performance of the TV model is the primary goal of the GA2AD
framework, considering the trade-off on efficiency and comfort (i.e., multi-objective
optimization with safety as the primary concern). Four metrics, namely, the collision
rate, environmental risk, average speed and the 95th percentile absolute jerk, are
used to evaluate the performance of the TV model on robustness, which is defined as
maintaining safe, stable, and efficient driving under adversarial conditions.
The safety metrics like collision rate and environmental risk are inspired by ISO
26262 [52] and ISO 21448 [53] standards, which emphasize the importance of mini-
mizing hazard exposure. While direct measurement of injury severity is beyond the
scope of our study, reducing collision frequency and environmental risk serves as a
foundational step toward minimizing hazard exposure and enhancing AV robustness.
The effectiveness of the proposed approach in improving the safety performance of
AVs is evaluated via the collision rate, which quantifies the frequency of collisions per
kilometer traveled, serves as an indicator of TV safety [54]. Specifically, it is calculated
by dividing the total number of collisions by the total distance traveled during the
evaluation period. A lower collision rate corresponds to better safety performance and
greater robustness to potential disturbances or adversarial influences. The formula is
given by:

Simulation experimental setup
To evaluate the proposed methodology, we constructed an urban traffic scenario using 
the SUMO simulation platform based on real-world geographic data. The scenario fea-
tures a 3.2-kilometer mixed-geometry roadway with both straight and curved sections, 
four standard lanes, and three signalized intersections. A speed range of [0, 35] m/s was 
implemented to simulate high-speed traffic conditions. Vehicle maneuvers were defined 
in a 9-dimensional discretized action space, including left and right lane changes and 
seven discrete longitudinal acceleration values in [−4, 2] m/s2, with a resolution of 
1 m/s2. The BVs’ default maneuvers were generated by a rule-based model incorpo-
rating the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) [55] and the Minimizing Overall Braking 
Induced by Lane Changes (MOBIL) model [56], with calibrated parameters for acceler-
ation, desired speed, and lane-changing behavior to simulate human driving patterns.

An ABV was selected by an RL-based model from a 6-dimensional vehicle candidate
space to perform adversarial maneuvers, which can capture the randomness and het-
erogeneity of human driving behaviors and especially mimic extreme or unpredictable
human driving behaviors, through diverse and dynamic adversarial strategies. The TV
model, represented by a decision-making module, incorporated an RL algorithm with
a reward function emphasizing safety, efficiency, and stable driving behavior. The TV
model’s inputs included a 22-dimensional observation space comprising states of the
TV and surrounding BVs.
To consider behavioral diversity, heterogeneous algorithmic structures are
employed for the TV and BV. Specifically, the TV model is initially controlled by
a DQN algorithm [57], a classic off-policy and value-based reinforcement learning
method that serves as a baseline for comparisons with other RL-driven TV variants.
The RL-based BV model is trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
algorithm with key hyperparameters configured to balance learning efficiency and
adaptability [58]. More details and a reproducible experimental setup can be found in
Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Tables 1–3.
